## Abstract

**Background:** Plant protease inhibitors can impair lepidopteran pests, but they are large, and the short peptides derived from their reactive loops inhibit digestive trypsins only at sub-millimolar to millimolar concentrations. It is unknown whether natural inhibitors can guide *de novo* peptide design, and whether deep-learning scores can rank short designed peptides that the larval gut will not degrade. **Methods:** For eight lepidopteran soybean pests, including *Anticarsia gemmatalis*, crystal complexes of natural inhibitors defined the catalytic subsites, which were transferred to AlphaFold models; a scoring ladder (Boltz-2 co-folding, molecular dynamics, MM-GBSA) was calibrated with six natural inhibitors and shuffled decoys; and 22,066 peptide sequences were designed *de novo* on head-to-tail macrocyclic backbones (RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN). Sequences were screened with a motif score and a hard non-cleavability criterion (no P1 residue of midgut trypsin-, chymotrypsin- or elastase-like proteases); survivors are co-folded with Boltz-2 and simulated for 10 ns at pH 10.0 as linear peptides and as macrocycles. **Results:** Subsites transferred to all 20 receptor–template pairs (TM-score 0.946–0.957). Boltz-2 scored the natural inhibitor above its shuffled decoy in 10 of 10 pairs and ligand RMSD in 9 of 10, whereas MM-GBSA did so in 4 of 10 and tracked interface size (ρ = −0.93). The motif screen retained 1,829 sequences (Boltz-2 confidence 0.862, within the range of a shuffled SFTI-1 decoy, 0.944). The hard criterion retained 527 linear and 543 cyclic sequences (2.4–2.5%), 49% glycine, with 9 of 4,060 residues lysine or arginine, each before proline. Repeating Boltz-2 on identical inputs gave a between-run rank correlation of 0.57 (n = 442), close to the 0.50 between the linear and cyclic forms of the same sequence (n = 527). [[PENDING: paired controls, pose quality control and 10-ns simulations.]] **Conclusions:** Natural inhibitors served well as structural templates and calibration standards, but Boltz-2 confidence supports only pairwise comparisons and single-trajectory MM-GBSA should not rank such complexes. Requiring non-cleavability selects short, glycine-rich peptides that lack the residues favored at P1 by trypsin, which makes the trade-off between proteolytic resistance and S1 engagement explicit. All results are computational; selectivity and inhibitory activity were not tested.

## 1 Introduction

Lepidopteran larvae are among the main biotic factors limiting soybean productivity in South America, and the velvetbean caterpillar *Anticarsia gemmatalis* is one of the defoliating species of this group (Carpane et al., 2022; de Almeida Barros et al., 2021). In lepidopteran larvae, protein digestion takes place in a midgut lumen that is strongly alkaline; lepidopteran midguts generate the highest luminal pH known in a biological system (Dow, 1992). Trypsin-like enzymes are the main proteases of the *A. gemmatalis* midgut (de Almeida Barros et al., 2021), and serine proteases are the main protease class expressed in fifth-instar larvae (da Silva Júnior et al., 2020), and a proteomic survey of the larval intestine found 54 expressed protease antigens, indicating multiple isoforms (Silva-Júnior et al., 2021). General reviews of insect digestion describe the compartmentalization and properties of these enzymes (Terra and Ferreira, 1994).

Plants defend themselves against herbivores with proteinaceous protease inhibitors, many of them accumulated in seeds, and such inhibitors have been examined as leads for pest control. Examples that are relevant to trypsins of *A. gemmatalis* include the inhibitor from *Adenanthera pavonina* seeds (ApTI), which is a noncompetitive tight-binding inhibitor of larval trypsins (Meriño-Cabrera et al., 2020), and the bovine pancreatic and soybean Kunitz inhibitors, which reduced larval survival with distinct effects on the insect proteolytic response (de Almeida Barros et al., 2022). At the small end of the size range, the 14-residue cyclic peptide SFTI-1 from sunflower seeds inhibits trypsin with a K~i~ of 100 pM, and its potency has been attributed to the rigidity conferred by cyclization and a single disulfide bond (Luckett et al., 1999). A recurrent limitation is insect adaptation: larvae compensate by inducing protease activity that is insensitive to the inhibitor (Jongsma et al., 1995; Jongsma and Bolter, 1997), by regulating the expression of multiple digestive proteases (Kuwar et al., 2015; Lomate et al., 2018), and, in some species, through gene-family expansion that may explain differences in sensitivity to soybean inhibitors between *Spodoptera frugiperda* and *Diatraea saccharalis* (Souza et al., 2016).

Natural inhibitors are also exposed to the insect midgut, where they can be degraded or bypassed, and peptides derived from their reactive-center loops (RCLs) have been proposed as smaller and more stable alternatives (Paulo et al., 2026). Short synthetic peptides that mimic the reactive-center loop of these inhibitors have therefore attracted interest, because they are cheaper to produce than proteins. Linear peptides derived from the interface of a Kunitz inhibitor–trypsin complex inhibited digestive trypsins of *Spodoptera cosmioides* and were toxic to the larvae (Meriño-Cabrera et al., 2020). Tripeptides derived from Pin-II inhibitors inhibited *Helicoverpa armigera* midgut proteases, with enhanced efficacy at alkaline gut pH (Saikhedkar et al., 2018), and bicyclic peptides built from the same loops were ten-fold more potent than their linear counterparts (Saikhedkar et al., 2019). In *A. gemmatalis*, the designed tripeptides GORE1 and GORE2 are reversible competitive inhibitors with K~i~ values of 0.49 and 0.10 mM, respectively, and impair larval survival and development (de Almeida Barros et al., 2021); their complexes were examined by molecular dynamics (de Almeida Barros et al., 2022), and arginine-containing dipeptides, predicted *in silico* to bind the S1 subsite of *A. gemmatalis* trypsins more strongly than lysine-containing peptides, were competitive inhibitors *in vitro* (Meriño-Cabrera et al., 2022). Peptides inspired by the reactive-center loops of BPTI and soybean Kunitz inhibitor were competitive inhibitors of *A. gemmatalis* trypsin-like proteases (Paulo et al., 2026), and GORE1 and GORE2 also inhibited trypsin-like proteases of *S. frugiperda* (Schultz et al., 2026). The GORE tripeptides have K~i~ values of 0.10–1.41 mM against trypsin-like proteases of *A. gemmatalis* and *S. frugiperda* (de Almeida Barros et al., 2021; Schultz et al., 2026), which leaves room for higher-affinity and more stable designs; a structure-guided design of an interface-derived peptide against *S. frugiperda* trypsins has also been reported (Severiche-Castro et al., 2026).

Canonical ("standard mechanism") inhibitors bind through an exposed loop of conserved conformation whose P1 residue inserts into the S1 pocket (Laskowski and Kato, 1980; Laskowski and Qasim, 2000); the remainder of the molecule, the scaffold, improves binding by about six orders of magnitude and protects the inhibitor from proteolysis (Kelly et al., 2005). For trypsin-like enzymes the S1 pocket is defined by an aspartate at the position numbered 189 in chymotrypsin, which favors lysine and arginine at P1 (Perona and Craik, 1995; Hedstrom, 2002); the trypsin-like enzymes of *A. gemmatalis* likewise show higher affinity for substrates with arginine at P1 (Patarroyo-Vargas et al., 2017). This raises a design tension that is central to the present study: the basic P1 residues that best engage S1 are themselves cleavage sites for the target enzyme.

A peptide that the larval gut cleaves cannot reach its target intact, so proteolytic stability is a requirement rather than an optimization. The lepidopteran midgut holds, besides trypsin-like enzymes, chymotrypsin-like and elastase-like endopeptidases and exopeptidases: luminal trypsin, elastase and a brush-border leucine aminopeptidase were purified from the gypsy moth (Valaitis, 1995), midgut proteinases of another lepidopteran were characterized in detail (Valaitis et al., 1999), chymotrypsin-like enzymes were cloned from *D. saccharalis* (Yang et al., 2012) and *S. litura* (Zhan et al., 2010), and carboxypeptidase A and aminopeptidase activities were measured in the midgut of a butterfly larva (Nakonieczny et al., 2007). A sequence that avoids the P1 residues of these enzymes (lysine and arginine for trypsin, aromatic and large aliphatic residues for chymotrypsin-like enzymes, small aliphatic residues for elastase-like enzymes) and, in a linear peptide, a cleavable C-terminus would in principle resist all of them, although the specificity of each enzyme in each of our species has not been measured. Head-to-tail cyclization is a complementary route, because a macrocycle has no free termini on which exopeptidases can act; linear and cyclic forms of the same sequence can therefore be compared under the same requirement.

Deep-learning methods now allow peptides and other binders to be generated *de novo*. RFdiffusion generates backbones conditioned on a target (Watson et al., 2023), ProteinMPNN assigns sequences to backbones (Dauparas et al., 2022), head-to-tail macrocycles can be designed with these tools (Rettie et al., 2025), and co-folding models such as Boltz-1 and Boltz-2 predict biomolecular complexes (Wohlwend et al., 2024; Passaro et al., 2025). Whether the confidence scores of such models rank short peptide candidates against insect proteases in a meaningful way has not been established, and end-point free-energy methods are known to be sensitive to sampling and to system type (Hou et al., 2011; Genheden and Ryde, 2015; Xu et al., 2025). A design campaign against insect proteases therefore needs an explicit calibration of the scoring steps it relies on.

Here we ask how far natural inhibitors can be carried into *de novo* peptide inhibitors. They supply the structural templates (the trypsin–inhibitor complexes that define the subsites), the calibration standards and the P1–S1 design logic; *de novo* design is asked to supply what they lack, namely small size and the absence of cleavage motifs. We report a computational pipeline that (i) defines the catalytic subsites of digestive trypsins from eight lepidopteran pests by structural transfer from crystal complexes of natural inhibitors, (ii) calibrates a scoring ladder against six natural inhibitors and shuffled decoys, (iii) generates head-to-tail macrocyclic backbones of 5–20 residues with RFdiffusion and ProteinMPNN, (iv) applies a motif-score screen and a hard non-cleavability criterion, and (v) co-folds the survivors with Boltz-2 and triages them by 10-ns molecular dynamics, in two fronts: the designed sequences as linear peptides and as head-to-tail macrocycles. An overview is given in Figure 1. The work is entirely computational. It does not evaluate selectivity against non-target proteases, which is a separate module of our project, and no result reported here should be read as evidence of inhibitory activity or selectivity.

## 2 Materials and methods

### 2.1 Target panel and receptor structures

The panel comprises digestive trypsin-like serine proteases of eight lepidopteran pests (*Spodoptera frugiperda*, *S. litura*, *Ostrinia nubilalis*, *Diatraea saccharalis*, *Chrysodeixis includens*, *Heliothis virescens*, *Plutella xylostella* and *Anticarsia gemmatalis*), *Manduca sexta* as the reference species with curated midgut evidence, and *Bombyx mori*, which was retained in the panel but not used as a design target. Only *M. sexta* (UniProt P35045–P35047) has reviewed entries annotated with midgut expression; for the other species the entries are unreviewed and were selected by homology, so the panel rests on sequence and structural evidence rather than on documented midgut expression. Structures are AlphaFold monomer v2.0 models (Jumper et al., 2021) retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (Varadi et al., 2024) (accessions in Table 1). Sequence identity to *M. sexta* alkaline trypsin B (P35046) was computed with Biopython (Cock et al., 2009) using a global alignment (BLOSUM62, gap open −11, gap extension −1; identical positions divided by the length of the shorter sequence), and per-model confidence is the mean pLDDT of the Cα atoms, read from the B-factor column.

Because UniProt names most of these entries "Chymotrypsin" (automatic annotation), trypsin-type specificity was checked from the sequence. The specificity residue was located six residues N-terminal to the catalytic serine, an offset calibrated on two reference proteins from UniProt (UniProt Consortium, 2025): bovine trypsin (P00760; catalytic Ser200, Asp at position 194 of the precursor) and bovine chymotrypsinogen A (P00766; Ser195, Ser at position 189). The catalytic serine of each panel sequence was found from the conserved motif G[DN]SGG[PT].

### 2.2 Definition and transfer of catalytic subsites

Subsites were defined from two crystal complexes of bovine trypsin obtained from the Protein Data Bank (Berman et al., 2000): trypsin–BPTI (2PTC, trypsin chain E) and trypsin–SFTI-1 (1SFI, trypsin chain A; Luckett et al., 1999). For each complex, the inhibitor residue whose carbonyl carbon lies closest to the Oγ of a serine of the trypsin chain was taken as P1 (2.68 Å for 2PTC and 2.83 Å for 1SFI; a distance above 4.5 Å would have failed the quality control). The seven inhibitor residues P4–P3′ (Schechter–Berger nomenclature; Schechter and Berger, 1967) define S4–S3′, and a trypsin residue was assigned to a subsite when any of its atoms lay within 4.5 Å of the corresponding inhibitor residue; trypsin residues within 4.5 Å of any other inhibitor residue were assigned to an exosite. Each trypsin chain was then structurally aligned with each panel receptor using Foldseek (van Kempen et al., 2024) in TM-align mode (`--alignment-type 1`, exhaustive search), which implements the TM-align algorithm (Zhang and Skolnick, 2005), and reference residues were transferred through the residue-to-residue correspondence of the alignment. A receptor–template pair was accepted when the alignment TM-score (Foldseek `alntmscore`, normalized by alignment length) was ≥0.5 and the RMSD ≤3.0 Å.

### 2.3 Calibration of the scoring ladder

Six natural inhibitors of known sequence served as positive controls. Mature sequences were taken from the PDB entries (Berman et al., 2000) of bovine pancreatic trypsin inhibitor (BPTI; UniProt P00974; 1BPI, Parkin et al., 1996; 58 residues), SFTI-1 (Q4GWU5; 1SFI, Luckett et al., 1999; 14 residues, modeled as the linear sequence), soybean Kunitz trypsin inhibitor (SKTI; P01070; 1AVU, Song and Suh, 1998; 172 residues), the Bowman–Birk inhibitor (BBI; P01055; 1BBI, Werner and Wemmer, 1992; 71 residues) and the *Enterolobium contortisiliquum* inhibitor (EcTI; P86451; 4J2K, chain A, Zhou et al., 2013; 168 residues); ApTI (P09941 and P09942; 176 residues in three chains) has no experimental structure and was taken from UniProt. For the first five, a decoy with the same amino-acid composition and a shuffled sequence (`random.seed(42)`) was generated. Each control and decoy was modeled with two receptors: bovine β-trypsin (the trypsin chain of 1SFI) and the *S. frugiperda* model A0A089QDB3, giving 22 systems in total (ApTI had no decoy).

The ladder had three rungs. (i) Co-folding with Boltz-2 (Passaro et al., 2025), using the checkpoint `boltz2_conf`, an MSA from the public ColabFold server (Mirdita et al., 2022), one diffusion sample and three recycling steps; the metrics were the Boltz-2 `confidence_score`, the complex pLDDT and the ipTM. (ii) A 2-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation started from each predicted complex (protocol in Section 2.8; pH 8.0 for bovine trypsin and 10.0 for *S. frugiperda*). The MD metric was the RMSD of the ligand backbone after superposition on the receptor Cα atoms, with the ligand made whole and translated to the periodic image nearest the receptor in every frame (necessary because the ligand and the receptor are separate molecules that can be written to different periodic images), averaged over the last third of the trajectory; the number of receptor residues with a heavy atom within 4.5 Å of the ligand was also recorded. The expected direction (real inhibitor with lower ligand RMSD and larger contact than its decoy) was fixed in the analysis script before it was run. (iii) End-point MM-GBSA on the same trajectories with gmx_MMPBSA (Valdés-Tresanco et al., 2021) and AmberTools 24.8 (generalized Born model `igb=5`, 0.150 M salt; 45 frames from the last third of each trajectory, one trajectory per complex, no entropy term). A method was said to separate real from decoy in a pair when the real inhibitor had the more favorable value. Because each decoy is shared by the two receptors, the ten pairs are not independent.

### 2.4 Generation of macrocyclic backbones

Backbones were generated with RFdiffusion (package 1.1.0; Watson et al., 2023) using `Complex_base_ckpt`, 50 diffusion steps, `denoiser.noise_scale_ca=0.2`, `noise_scale_frame=0.1` and random seeds, in head-to-tail cyclic mode (`inference.cyclic=True`, `cyc_chains=a`), with the receptor chain fixed (contig `A1–N/0 L–L` for a receptor of N residues and a peptide of L residues). For each of the eight targets, ten backbones were generated for each of 11 lengths (5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 19 and 20 residues), i.e. 110 per species and 880 in total. Hotspot residues came from the S1 and S2 subsites transferred from the trypsin–SFTI-1 template (15 residues per receptor); the RFdiffusion interface accepted only the first eight of these in ascending residue number, so the hotspots effectively used were the equivalents of His57, Leu99, Asp189, Ser190, Cys191, Gln192, Gly193 and Asp194 (bovine numbering). The equivalents of the catalytic Ser195 and of the S1 wall residues 213–216, 219 and 226 were not used as hotspots. Ring closure was checked as the distance between the N atom of the first residue and the C atom of the last residue of the peptide chain.

### 2.5 Sequence design

Sequences were assigned to each backbone with ProteinMPNN (commit 8907e66; Dauparas et al., 2022) using the weights `v_48_020`, sampling temperature 0.1, backbone noise 0.05, cysteine and unknown residues excluded, 30 sequences per backbone and random seeds. The program was run with its default chain settings, in which all chains are designed (`designed_chains=['A','B']`); the receptor sequence was therefore redesigned together with the peptide and was not held fixed, and only the peptide chain was retained. Identical sequences were merged within each species, and a merged sequence was kept with the first backbone that generated it. No amino-acid restrictions were imposed at design time; proteolytic liability was handled by the post hoc screen below.

### 2.6 Cleavage screens

Two screens were applied to the 22,066 sequences. *(i) Motif-score screen (first round).* Each sequence was scanned with seven simplified P1-motif rules, defined with reference to the enzyme specificities compiled in PeptideCutter (Gasteiger et al., 2005) but simplified and not benchmarked against it: trypsin (after K/R, not before P), chymotrypsin at high (F/Y/W) and low (F/Y/W/M/L) specificity, an elastase-like rule (after A/G/S/V, not before P), Lys-C, Arg-C and a pepsin-like rule; the last is not a lepidopteran gut protease and nevertheless contributes to the score with the weight of a priority-2 enzyme. Because the candidates are head-to-tail macrocycles, the peptide bond between the last and the first residue was evaluated like any other (a "circular" scan). The residue of the peptide whose Cα atom lay closest to the Cα of the catalytic serine in the RFdiffusion backbone was taken as a geometric proxy for P1; if that residue was a trypsin site, it was exempted from the count of internal trypsin sites, otherwise every trypsin site counted as internal. A susceptibility score was computed as the weighted number of sites (weights 1.0, 0.6 and 0.3 for protease priorities 1, 2 and 3; for trypsin only internal sites were counted, for the other rules all sites), divided by 10 and capped at 1. A sequence was labelled RESISTENTE (resistant-like) with no internal trypsin site and a score below 0.3, MARGINAL with at most one internal site and a score below 0.5, and SUSCEPTIVEL otherwise. These labels are motif-based predictions and do not measure proteolysis. They are also sensitive to a single site: for example, GIFDDIG has no trypsin site but a score of 0.34 and is labelled MARGINAL.

*(ii) Hard non-cleavability criterion.* The requirement that the peptide not be cleaved by midgut proteases is a constraint rather than a weighted score, so a binary criterion was added, with the P1 residues given in Figure 2. A sequence was kept only if no residue of the chain is K or R (trypsin-like), F, Y, W, L or M (chymotrypsin-like), or A or V (elastase-like; L and M already included), unless the following residue is proline. In the linear peptide the C-terminal residue is also evaluated, because its free carboxylate makes it a substrate of carboxypeptidases: it must not belong to the set above and must not be isoleucine. In the macrocycle there are no termini and the closing bond is evaluated like any other, so that a residue preceding the first residue is protected when the first residue is proline. No exemption is made for the residue closest to the catalytic serine. Isoleucine is allowed in the interior of the chain, since we found no evidence that it is a P1 residue of these enzymes; a sensitivity analysis also forbids it. The rule is applied to the same 22,066 sequences for the linear (front L) and cyclic (front M) interpretation, and labels are still called RESISTENTE (resistant-like). The criterion is a prediction at motif level. It rests on the specificities reported for lepidopteran midgut enzymes (Valaitis, 1995; Valaitis et al., 1999; Yang et al., 2012; Zhan et al., 2010; Nakonieczny et al., 2007), it treats each exception of the K/R–Pro type as an approximation, and it cannot remove the action of aminopeptidase N, which needs a free N-terminus and is avoided only by cyclization or by capping the peptide.

### 2.7 Boltz-2 co-folding of candidates

*First round.* Candidates labelled RESISTENTE were co-folded with each receptor using Boltz-2 (version 2.2.1; Passaro et al., 2025) with the receptor sequence and a receptor MSA computed once per species against the public ColabFold server (Mirdita et al., 2022), and the peptide declared cyclic (`cyclic: true`) with no MSA. One prediction was made per candidate with default settings (`--preprocessing-threads 4`). We report the Boltz-2 `confidence_score`, which we verified to equal 0.8·pLDDT + 0.2·ipTM for all predictions (largest absolute deviation 8 × 10^−8^), complex pLDDT and ipTM. Boltz-2 affinity outputs were not used.

*Two-front screen (E1).* Candidates satisfying the hard criterion were co-folded with the same protocol in two modalities: as linear peptides (`cyclic: false`, front L) and as head-to-tail macrocycles (`cyclic: true`, front M). The M front re-predicts candidates of the first round and therefore also tests the reproducibility of Boltz-2 for an identical input. *Robust re-scoring (E2).* The ten candidates with the highest confidence per species and front were re-predicted with five diffusion samples, three recycling steps, 200 sampling steps, inference-time potentials (`--use_potentials`) and three random seeds; the score of a candidate is the mean over all predictions and the best single prediction is the starting structure for the simulation. *Paired controls (E3).* For each of these candidates, three shuffled sequences with the same composition and length (seed fixed by the sequence) were predicted with the same protocol, and the paired difference Δ = score of the candidate − mean score of its controls was computed, because the calibration supports comparisons within pairs and not absolute values (Section 4.2). *Pose quality control (E4).* Thresholds were fixed before the predictions were analyzed: no heavy-atom pair between peptide and receptor closer than 2.2 Å; |ω| ≥ 150° for peptide bonds (Pro cis accepted); L chirality at every Cα (Gly excluded); His57 Nε2–Ser195 Oγ distance ≤ 3.8 Å (receptor-equivalent residues); and, for macrocycles, closing C–N distance ≤ 1.5 Å with closing ω ≥ 150°. The top-ranked candidate of each species was also co-folded with the seven other receptors (8 × 8 cross matrix). The three top-ranked candidates per species and front, taken as distinct sequences by mean confidence among the resistant-like candidates, were passed to the simulations; Δ is interpreted afterwards and is not used for selection.

### 2.8 Molecular dynamics of screening (10 ns)

Each of the three top candidates per species and front (24 linear and 24 cyclic complexes) was simulated for 10 ns as a screening step; the authors run longer simulations on the candidates that pass. Starting from the Boltz-2 complex, protonation of side chains at pH 10.0 was assigned with PROPKA 3 (Olsson et al., 2011) through PDB2PQR 3.6.2 (Dolinsky et al., 2007). This value was chosen because it is the most compatible with the midgut of lepidopteran larvae: extracts of the *H. virescens* gut had pH 9.56–10.0 (Karumbaiah et al., 2007), and lepidopteran midguts reach the highest luminal pH known (Dow, 1992). Systems were assembled with GROMACS 2025.4 (Abraham et al., 2015) and the CHARMM36 additive protein force field (Huang and MacKerell, 2013) (GROMACS port of February 2026 generated with charmm2gmx (Wacha and Lemkul, 2023), the same force field used in the other trypsin–peptide simulations of our group) with TIP3P water (Jorgensen et al., 1983), in a dodecahedral box with a 1.2-nm buffer and 0.10 M KCl (potassium is the dominant cation of insect hemolymph). The linear peptide has NH~3~^+^ and COO^−^ termini, the CHARMM36 default; at pH 10 the α-amino group (pKa ≈ 8) would be mostly neutral, so this is a limitation that affects only the linear front. The assembly script checks that the C(n)–N(1) bond and the CMAP term of every residue are present and stops otherwise, so that a ring never runs as an open linear chain. [[PENDING: closing C–N distance and ring ω in the 24 cyclic systems when all have finished; Section 3.9 and Figure S2 give the three finished so far.]] Steps and controls were identical in both fronts: steepest-descent minimization, 200 ps of NVT and 500 ps of NPT equilibration with position restraints on protein heavy atoms (1,000 kJ mol^−1^ nm^−2^), then 10 ns of production at 300 K and 1 bar with the velocity-rescaling thermostat (τ = 0.1 ps; Bussi et al., 2007), the Parrinello–Rahman barostat (τ = 2 ps; Parrinello and Rahman, 1981), particle-mesh Ewald electrostatics (Essmann et al., 1995), a 1.2-nm Coulomb cutoff, van der Waals forces switched off between 1.0 and 1.2 nm (force-switch, without dispersion correction, as the CHARMM36 parametrization requires), constrained bonds to hydrogen (LINCS) and a 2-fs time step. Each system was simulated once with a random seed for the initial velocities; ten nanoseconds with one replicate is a descriptive screen and no statistical inference is made.

### 2.9 Trajectory analysis

Trajectories were made whole and centered on the protein (`gmx trjconv -pbc mol -center`) and subsampled. This step is required because the peptide is a separate molecule: in the uncorrected trajectory of one of the systems of an earlier 50-ns round of this project the distance between the centers of mass of receptor and peptide reached 94.8 Å (median 44.7 Å, box edge 116 Å) although the peptide remained in contact with the receptor in every frame. Analyses used MDAnalysis 2.9.0 (Michaud-Agrawal et al., 2011; Gowers et al., 2016). The S1 aspartate was the equivalent of Asp189 from Section 2.2 (its residue name was checked in every system). In each frame, the distance from every peptide residue to the carboxylate oxygens of that aspartate was taken as the minimum heavy-atom distance with the minimum-image convention. The peptide residue with the smallest mean distance was defined as the anchor, without assuming which residue it would be. S1 occupancy is the fraction of frames with anchor–Asp189 distance below 4, 5 or 6 Å, reported for the whole trajectory and separately for each half. The anchor–Asp189 distance is also reported for an initial window (the first 4% of the trajectory, 0.4 ns of 10 ns) and a final window (the last 20%, 2 ns of 10 ns); the same final window is used for the peptide RMSD reported at the end of the trajectory. We also report the fraction of frames in which any peptide heavy atom was within 4.5 Å of the Oγ of the catalytic serine or of the Nε2 of the catalytic histidine, the fraction of frames with any peptide–receptor contact within 4.5 Å, and the RMSD of the peptide Cα atoms after superposition on the receptor Cα atoms (reference: first production frame), computed after making the peptide whole and translating it to the periodic image nearest the S1 aspartate. For the macrocycle we also report the closing C–N distance and the closing ω angle in every frame. A candidate passes the screen (criteria declared before the simulations) when its anchor occupancy is at least 70% at 5 Å in the second half of the trajectory, the anchor is the same residue in both halves, and, for macrocycles, the ring stays intact (C–N ≤ 1.5 Å and ω ≥ 150° in every frame); because each candidate was simulated once, no statistical inference is made, and a candidate that fails is reported with the criterion it failed. After seeing the first cyclic trajectories we also report, as a secondary post hoc description that does not replace the criterion, the fraction of frames with |ω| ≥ 150°. The 48 candidates were finally ranked in tiers A–C (P while the simulation is pending) from the same criteria, without new thresholds (Section 3.12).

## 3 Results

### 3.1 The receptor panel: eight targets and two reference species

### 3.3 Calibration: Boltz-2 and ligand RMSD separate inhibitors from decoys, MM-GBSA does not

Boltz-2 gave a higher confidence for the real inhibitor than for its shuffled decoy in 10 of 10 pairs (differences of 0.042–0.274; median 0.190), and the same held for complex pLDDT (10/10; 0.026–0.312) and for ipTM (10/10; 0.005–0.399, the smallest margin being that of SKTI with *S. frugiperda*, 0.711 against 0.706). Absolute values overlapped, however: real inhibitors scored 0.850–0.986 and decoys 0.628–0.944, and the shuffled SFTI-1 decoy (14 residues) reached 0.944 with bovine trypsin (Table 3). Discrimination therefore holds within a pair but not as an absolute threshold (Figure 3A).

The ligand RMSD after superposition on the receptor (2 ns, last third) was lower for the real inhibitor than for its decoy in 9 of 10 pairs (Figure 3B); the exception was *S. frugiperda*–EcTI (0.366 versus 0.347 nm). The number of receptor residues in contact with the ligand was larger for the real inhibitor in only 2 of 10 pairs.

MM-GBSA favored the real inhibitor in only 4 of 10 pairs; in the other six the decoy was more favorable, by up to 144.8 kcal mol^−1^ (Table 3). Across the 22 systems, ΔG correlated with the number of receptor residues in contact with the ligand (Spearman ρ = −0.93, *P* = 7 × 10^−10^; a post hoc analysis), whereas it did not correlate with ligand length (ρ = −0.27, *P* = 0.23). In this single-trajectory 2-ns setting, MM-GBSA behaved as a measure of interface size (Figure 3C, D) and was not used for decisions. In one system (*S. frugiperda*–BPTI) the ligand was found in a different periodic image of the receptor in at least one frame of the trajectory used for MM-GBSA, which is a further reason not to interpret the absolute values.

### 3.4 The generation campaign

Generation produced 110 macrocyclic backbones per species (10 per length, 11 lengths), 880 in total. In all of them the chain of the peptide had the requested length, and the N–C closure distance was 0.76–1.40 Å (none above 2 Å). These values indicate that the closure constraint was met within the tolerance of the backbone model; the backbones were not relaxed at all-atom level, and bond geometry was not validated. ProteinMPNN yielded 22,066 unique sequences (2,692–2,839 per species, Table 4), all with the length of their backbone. The hotspot residues effectively passed to RFdiffusion were those listed in Section 2.4 and can be read from the `.trb` file of every backbone.

### 3.5 The motif-score screen selects short peptides without basic residues

With the circular scan, 1,829 of 22,066 sequences (8.3%) were labelled RESISTENTE, 4,987 (22.6%) MARGINAL and 15,250 (69.1%) SUSCEPTIVEL (Table 4 and Figure 4). The screen mainly removed long sequences: the RESISTENTE set had a mean length of 7.05 residues and 95.6% of its members had 10 residues or fewer, against a mean of 15.74 residues and 12.7% for the SUSCEPTIVEL set (Figure 4A). Only 4.3% of the RESISTENTE sequences contained any lysine or arginine, against 85% of the SUSCEPTIVEL ones, and in only 1.3% did the geometric P1 proxy fall on a lysine or arginine. Glycine (19.5%), threonine (14.1%), proline (13.3%), serine (9.8%) and aspartate (9.3%) were the most frequent residues of the RESISTENTE set, and arginine and lysine together accounted for 0.7%.

### 3.6 A hard non-cleavability criterion leaves 527 linear and 543 cyclic candidates

Applying the hard criterion (Section 2.6; Figure 2) to the 22,066 sequences left 527 sequences (2.4%) as resistant-like for the linear peptide and 543 (2.5%) for the macrocycle, 41–86 and 41–87 per species respectively (Figure 5). All 527 linear sequences are also in the cyclic set. Of the 16 cyclic-only sequences, 14 end in a residue that is forbidden at P1 but is followed, across the ring closure, by a proline that is also the first residue (for example PSDDPEW and PISQIDSGSR), and two end in isoleucine, which is forbidden only at a free C-terminus. The hard set and the 1,829 sequences of the motif-score screen do not nest: 396 of the 543 cyclic candidates (73%) were also resistant-like by the motif score, because the score tolerated sites that the hard criterion does not and penalized some sequences that the hard criterion accepts.

The hard set is short and rich in glycine (Figure 6). The mean length was 7.7 residues, 430 of the 527 linear sequences (81.6%) had 8 residues or fewer, and the range was 5–20. Glycine made up 49.4% of the 4,060 residues of the linear set, followed by serine (11.7%), proline (9.8%), threonine (9.0%), aspartate (5.8%), isoleucine (3.9%) and asparagine (3.8%). Lysine or arginine accounted for 9 residues (0.22%), every one followed by proline (six Arg–Pro and three Lys–Pro). If isoleucine is also forbidden in the interior of the chain, 393 linear sequences remain. Of the eight top candidates of the first round (Table 4), only NSNPNNPN (*S. litura*), NNGGG (*C. includens*) and GGKPGEP (*A. gemmatalis*) satisfy the criterion; GGKPGEP passes because its lysine precedes a proline.

### 3.7 Boltz-2 confidence of the first-round candidates

The 1,829 resistant-like candidates were co-folded with their receptors (Section 2.7). The Boltz-2 confidence score had a mean of 0.862 (median 0.868; range 0.695–0.953), with 345 candidates (18.9%) at 0.90 or above and 1,658 (90.7%) at 0.80 or above; mean ipTM was 0.753 and mean complex pLDDT 0.889. Confidence depended little on length (mean 0.866 for 5-residue and 0.861 for 10-residue candidates, 0.840 for 12 residues; Spearman ρ = −0.07, *P* = 0.0015; only 8 candidates were longer than 12 residues). These values overlap those of the natural inhibitors of the calibration (real inhibitors 0.850–0.986; 1,189 candidates, 65.0%, scored ≥0.850) and of its shuffled decoys (0.628–0.944; 8 candidates scored ≥0.944, the value reached by the shuffled SFTI-1 decoy). Because a shuffled 14-residue decoy can reach 0.944 (Section 3.3), a confidence of 0.90–0.95 for a peptide of 5–8 residues is not by itself evidence of binding. Per-species means ranged from 0.814 (*S. litura*, where no candidate reached 0.90) to 0.887 (*D. saccharalis*, 99 candidates ≥0.90). The top-scoring candidate of each species (Table 4) was 5–8 residues long and, in seven of eight species, free of lysine and arginine; the exception is GGKPGEP of *A. gemmatalis* (confidence 0.939, ipTM 0.976), which is also the highest-scoring of the 78 candidates that contain lysine or arginine. Figure 7 shows the distributions. Of the 543 cyclic candidates of the hard criterion, 442 already had a first-round prediction (mean confidence 0.864), and the remaining 101 are predicted in the two-front screen (Section 3.8).

### 3.8 Two-front co-folding, paired controls and pose quality

*Two-front screen (E1).* All 527 linear and 543 cyclic candidates were predicted (1,070 predictions; one cyclic prediction failed in pre-processing and was repeated). Mean confidence was 0.874 for the linear and 0.862 for the cyclic form. Predicting 442 cyclic candidates a second time with identical input gave a rank correlation of only 0.57 between runs (mean absolute difference 0.028; 0.21–0.69 by species; Figure 8A). The linear and cyclic predictions of the same sequence (n = 527) correlated at 0.50 (mean absolute difference 0.033; 0.16–0.44 by species), and the linear form was higher by 0.013 on average (Figure 8B). The rank agreement between the two modalities is therefore close to the agreement between two runs of the same modality, and a single prediction cannot separate an effect of the modality from sampling noise. The same noise affects the choice of the ten highest candidates per species from E1, which is why E2 repeats each of them with five samples and three seeds. The three highest candidates of the E1 ranking shared at least one sequence between the two fronts in four of the eight species and none in the other four; these are not the final selections. In a post hoc look at the single-sample predictions of the linear front, 96 of 527 (18%) passed the pose quality control of Section 2.7, and the main cause of failure was a peptide–receptor heavy-atom contact closer than 2.2 Å (427 predictions; median minimum distance 1.85 Å), which the inference-time potentials used from E2 onwards are meant to reduce.

*Re-scoring (E2) and pose quality (E4).* The ten highest-confidence candidates per species in each front (80 per front) were predicted again with five diffusion samples, three seeds and the inference-time potentials (15 predictions each). Relative to the single E1 prediction, mean confidence fell from 0.922 to 0.900 in the linear front and from 0.911 to 0.888 in the cyclic front (Δ = −0.021 and −0.023), the regression expected for a set selected for its top rank; the rank correlation between E1 and E2 was 0.80 and 0.74 (Figure 9A). The standard deviation among the 15 predictions of one candidate averaged 0.019 (linear) and 0.022 (cyclic), of the same order as the spread among the ten candidates of a species (Figure 9B), so the order inside a top ten is not resolved. The two top-ten sets share 22 sequences, for which the correlation between the linear and the cyclic E2 confidence was 0.60 and the cyclic form was lower by 0.016 on average. Every candidate had at least one of its 15 samples passing the pose quality control of Section 2.7, and on average 98% (linear) and 95% (cyclic) of the samples passed (Figure 9C; one cyclic candidate had fewer than half). This proportion is not comparable with the 18% of the post hoc look at E1, because E2 concerns the 80 best-scored candidates per front and uses the potentials. The three final candidates per species and front (24 linear and 24 cyclic; highest mean E2 confidence among distinct resistant sequences, Figure 10A) all passed the quality control, with the catalytic triad intact (His57–Ser195 distance 2.4–3.4 Å) and no peptide–receptor heavy-atom pair closer than 2.4 Å. The starting poses differ in how close the peptide sits to the S1 aspartate: the minimum distance to the Asp189 carboxylate was ≤5 Å in 9 of 24 linear and 18 of 24 cyclic starting structures (medians 5.5 and 4.3 Å; Figure 10B). Only three of the 48 selected candidates contain lysine or arginine (linear NGGRPDAP; cyclic PISQIDSGSR and GGKPGEP).

With one 10-ns replicate per candidate, these are descriptions of single trajectories.

*Reference scale for the S1 criterion.* The 70% occupancy threshold was declared without knowing what a real inhibitor scores under the same analysis, so we applied the identical analysis to the calibration complexes, which use the same *S. frugiperda* receptor: six natural inhibitors and five shuffled decoys. Size-matched controls at the same protocol — the shuffled controls of the candidates themselves, simulated for 10 ns — are being run and will be reported with the final tiers.

## 4 Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

We assembled and calibrated a pipeline that goes from a panel of eight lepidopteran digestive trypsins to triaged candidate peptides, and we made non-cleavability by midgut proteases an explicit, binary requirement. The structural part was robust: catalytic subsites defined from two crystal complexes were transferred to all ten receptor models with high structural agreement, and the Asp189-equivalent found by structure agreed with the one found from the sequence. The candidate part yielded 22,066 unique sequences from 880 macrocyclic backbones, of which 1,829 passed a motif-score screen and were co-folded with Boltz-2 in a first round, and 527 (linear) and 543 (cyclic) satisfied the hard criterion. [[MD-DEPENDENT SENTENCE: outcome of the two-front screen and of the 10-ns simulations, to be written when Sections 3.8–3.10 are complete]]. No result here bears on inhibitory activity or on selectivity, because no assay and no counter-selection against non-target proteases was performed.

What the natural inhibitors contributed, and what the *de novo* peptides lack, is part of the answer to the question that frames this work. Natural inhibitors supplied the catalytic subsites (2PTC and 1SFI), the calibration standards and the S1-anchoring logic, and these parts of the pipeline worked. They did not supply their defining structural feature: the designed peptides contain no cysteine, so the disulfide-stabilized loops of BPTI, SKTI and SFTI-1, and the rigidity they confer, are not reproduced; the head-to-tail ring is the only constraint retained, and in only one of the two fronts. Rigidity is also not simply an advantage against adapted lepidopteran trypsins: a Bowman–Birk inhibitor with seven disulfide bonds inhibited partially purified *A. gemmatalis* trypsin-like enzymes less than the soybean Kunitz inhibitor, which its authors attributed to lower flexibility and to an evolutionary increase in the hydrophobicity of the subsites (Patarroyo-Vargas et al., 2020).

### 4.2 What the calibration does and does not support

Boltz-2 preferred the real inhibitor to its shuffled decoy in every pair, but the absolute scores of real inhibitors and decoys overlapped, and a shuffled 14-residue decoy of SFTI-1 scored 0.944. The candidates of the campaign are 5–8 residues long and were mostly scored between 0.80 and 0.95; the calibration therefore supports comparing a candidate with a matched control, and does not support reading a confidence of 0.9 as evidence of binding. Two properties of the calibration limit its reach further. First, the decoys are shuffled sequences of folded proteins, so they differ from the real inhibitors in folding as well as in binding; a low confidence for a decoy may reflect the absence of a fold rather than the absence of a complementary surface. Second, the calibration ligands have 14–176 residues, whereas the candidates have 5–8 residues, and no calibration was done with short peptides of known activity. The pairs are also not independent, since each decoy is used with two receptors.

The ligand RMSD after superposition on the receptor separated 9 of 10 pairs, with an expected direction fixed beforehand. This metric rewards a ligand that stays where the co-folding model placed it, and a decoy predicted with lower confidence may drift for that reason alone; the result should be read as agreement between the stability of the pose in a 2-ns simulation and the confidence of the co-folding model, not as an independent measure of affinity. MM-GBSA from a single 2-ns trajectory without an entropy term ranked the real inhibitor first in only 4 of 10 pairs and correlated with the number of receptor residues in contact with the ligand (ρ = −0.93). This is consistent with the general sensitivity of end-point methods to sampling and system type (Hou et al., 2011; Genheden and Ryde, 2015; Xu et al., 2025), and it means that MM-GBSA in this configuration reports the size of the interface. It was therefore not used to rank candidates.

A methodological point deserves emphasis. Separate receptor and ligand molecules can be written to different periodic images of the simulation box; in one of our systems the center-of-mass distance between receptor and peptide reached 94.8 Å in a 116-Å box while the two molecules were in contact in every frame. Any RMSD or contact analysis of protein–peptide simulations that does not correct for this will report displacement of the box image as instability, and the correction should be verified explicitly when a triage relies on such metrics.

The run-to-run rank correlation of 0.57 (Section 3.8) adds a further limit. A single Boltz-2 prediction of a short peptide is a noisy ranker even for identical input, so differences of a few hundredths in confidence, which separate many candidates, should not be interpreted, and the agreement between linear and cyclic forms (ρ = 0.50) cannot be read as a modality effect until it is compared with that ceiling.

### 4.3 Proteolytic resistance and S1 engagement pull in opposite directions

The cleavage-motif screen removed 91.7% of the sequences and selected peptides that are short (mean 7.05 residues; 95.6% with ten residues or fewer) and almost free of lysine and arginine (4.3%); only 1.3% had a lysine or arginine at the position closest to the catalytic serine. For standard-mechanism inhibitors the P1 residue inserts into S1 (Laskowski and Kato, 1980; Laskowski and Qasim, 2000), and trypsin-like enzymes, including those of *A. gemmatalis*, prefer arginine or lysine there (Patarroyo-Vargas et al., 2017; Meriño-Cabrera et al., 2022). The screen thus selects mainly for sequences that lack the residue best suited to engage the S1 aspartate. This follows from the definition of the class, which requires no internal trypsin site: a basic residue not followed by proline, at any position other than the geometric P1, counts as a site, and long sequences accumulate further motif hits. The alternative offered by the literature is rigidity: the scaffold of canonical inhibitors improves binding by about six orders of magnitude and protects against proteolysis (Kelly et al., 2005), and SFTI-1, whose P1 is a lysine in the complex we used (Section 3.2), is a potent trypsin inhibitor owing to a cyclic, disulfide-stabilized structure (Luckett et al., 1999). A rigid macrocycle may therefore tolerate one basic P1 that a linear motif rule would flag. The 78 candidates that contain lysine or arginine, among them the top candidate of *A. gemmatalis* (GGKPGEP), form a small set in which this possibility can be examined; whether their conformation in solution keeps that residue protected is not something the present screen can tell.

The hard criterion makes the same trade-off explicit and more severe. By forbidding every residue that a trypsin-, chymotrypsin- or elastase-like enzyme can take at P1, it leaves peptides that are almost half glycine (49.4% of the residues) and contain only nine basic residues, all protected by a following proline. Such sequences are likely to be flexible, which may cost binding entropy, and they lack the side chains that define the S1 interaction of canonical inhibitors. The only basic residues that survive are followed by proline, an exception that we took from the general specificity of the enzymes and that is an approximation for each species. GGKPGEP, the top candidate of *A. gemmatalis* in the first round, is an example: its lysine is protected by the next residue and could still reach S1. Whether any such sequence binds, and binds through the expected anchor, is what the simulations and the paired controls are meant to address, and a negative outcome for a species would be informative in itself. Cyclization removes the exposed termini but not the interior sites, so the macrocycle gained only 16 sequences over the linear set. Aminopeptidase N, which needs a free N-terminus, is not addressed by any sequence rule for the linear peptides; capping the N-terminus, which would also change the protonation state that the force field cannot represent, would be the practical answer.

The motif rules are also coarse. The score is a weighted count of sites, the elastase-like rule flags four common residues (alanine, glycine, serine and valine), the pepsin-like rule is not relevant to the lepidopteran gut, and a class can change with one site (GIFDDIG has no trypsin site and is nevertheless labelled MARGINAL). The RESISTENTE label is a computational prediction of low motif burden and not a measurement of stability in the midgut.

### 4.4 Limitations

(i) All results are computational; no inhibition or stability assay was performed. (ii) Selectivity was not addressed: the S1 pocket is conserved among trypsins, and a peptide anchored there cannot be assumed to spare non-target proteases; a counter-selection against non-target receptors is planned and was not built. (iii) The receptors are AlphaFold models, and for none of the eight pest species is there curated evidence of midgut expression. (iv) ProteinMPNN was run with all chains designed, so the receptor sequence was not fixed while the peptide was designed; only the peptide was kept and it was evaluated against the native receptor. (v) Only eight of the 15 S1/S2 hotspot residues reached RFdiffusion, and the catalytic serine and the walls of S1 were not among them. (vi) The sequences were designed on cyclic backbones; the linear front evaluates the same sequences without redesign, so it is not a design of linear peptides. Ring closure was met within the tolerance of the backbone model (0.76–1.40 Å). (vii) The non-cleavability criterion is a motif-level prediction: the specificity of each enzyme in each species is not measured, the K/R–Pro exception is an approximation, and aminopeptidase N is not excluded for linear peptides. (viii) The simulations are single 10-ns replicates of a legacy force field at pH 10.0; the linear peptide carries NH~3~^+^/COO^−^ termini although the α-amino group would be mostly neutral at that pH; ten nanoseconds only screen, and they cannot establish stability or rank candidates. (ix) A single Boltz-2 prediction is a noisy ranker (between-run ρ = 0.57 for identical input), so the selection of candidates for re-scoring rests on a noisy ranking. (x) The 22 calibration systems were simulated for 2 ns from a single predicted starting structure each, and the calibration ligands are much larger than the candidates.

(ix) The S1 occupancy criterion has no demonstrated specificity: in the only reference set available every shuffled decoy satisfied it (Section 3.9). (x) The anchor is defined post hoc as the peptide residue with the smallest mean distance to Asp189, and its occupancy is then scored, which favours the measured value over a residue fixed in advance. (xi) Ten nanoseconds starting from the predicted complex test whether that pose is stable, not whether the peptide can find S1: the candidates that passed started within 3.5 Å of Asp189, while those starting at 4.1–4.3 Å did not. (xii) The shuffled controls of E3 are weak for the glycine-rich candidates, because shuffling barely changes such a sequence: the mean identity between a candidate and its own controls is 0.35 (linear) and 0.29 (cyclic), five candidates are at least 60% identical to their controls, and six have only two distinct controls out of the three generated. (xiii) The criterion for those controls, Δ > 0, carries no uncertainty threshold, whereas the standard deviation among the fifteen predictions of a single candidate is about 0.02, so a small positive Δ is within noise. (xiv) Two deviations from current practice were found in the simulation set-up and kept so that every simulation shares one protocol: the NPT equilibration used the Parrinello-Rahman barostat, where a relaxation barostat is standard, and `refcoord_scaling` was not set with position restraints under pressure coupling; measured over three systems, density stayed at 1022–1033 kg m^−3^ with a root-mean-square fluctuation of 0.2% and negligible drift, so neither produced a detectable artefact.

### 4.5 Outlook

The most direct improvements follow from the limitations: fixing the receptor sequence during design and allowing exactly one basic P1 residue followed by proline at design time, rather than filtering afterwards; designing linear backbones directly; capping or neutralizing the termini of the linear peptides; replicate simulations of the candidates that pass, extended to hundreds of nanoseconds with a modern force field; and a counter-selection panel of non-target proteases so that selectivity becomes an explicit objective. The decisive test is enzymatic: inhibition assays with midgut extracts of *A. gemmatalis* and with non-target trypsins, and a stability assay of the candidates in the same extracts.
